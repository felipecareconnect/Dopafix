How to use this kit

Your phone is not the enemy. It is a tool that has been engineered to capture your attention. The problem is not that you are weak. The problem is that your brain is fighting an army of designers whose full-time job is to keep you scrolling.

The goal of this kit

Not to eliminate phone use. Not to shame you for screen time. The goal is to create intentional boundaries — spaces and times where your phone serves you, and spaces and times where it does not.

What is in this kit

  • The Phone-Use Agreement — A personal contract with yourself about when, where, and how you use your phone.
  • The Screen-Time Checklist — A daily or weekly audit to notice patterns without judgment.
  • Distraction-Interruption Cards — Physical cards to place around your workspace as visual reminders and friction creators.
  • The App Audit — A structured review of which apps serve you and which ones drain you.
  • Emergency Protocols — For when you pick up your phone without thinking and need a way back.

How to start

  1. Pick ONE boundary to implement this week. Just one.
  2. Print the relevant cards and place them where you will see them.
  3. Complete the Phone-Use Agreement. Sign it. Post it somewhere visible.
  4. Use the Screen-Time Checklist for three days. Notice, don't judge.
"The goal is not to use your phone less. The goal is to use your phone on purpose."

The Phone-Use Agreement

A personal contract with yourself about when, where, and how you use your phone.

The phone-use agreement

Willpower is unreliable. Boundaries are not. This agreement exists because decisions made in advance are easier to keep than decisions made in the moment.

How to write your agreement

  1. Be specific. "Use my phone less" is not a boundary. "No phone in bed" is.
  2. Be realistic. If you set a boundary you cannot keep, you will abandon the whole system.
  3. Be kind. This is not a punishment. It is a support structure.
  4. Be visible. Post this agreement where you will see it daily.
Agreement categories

Time boundaries: Specific hours when the phone is off-limits (meals, first hour after waking, last hour before sleep).

Space boundaries: Physical locations where the phone does not go (bedroom, dinner table, desk during focus blocks).

Activity boundaries: Activities where the phone is not invited (conversations, walks, creative work, reading).

Important: This agreement is with yourself. You can renegotiate it anytime. If a boundary isn't working, change it. The agreement serves you. You do not serve the agreement.

The Screen-Time Checklist

A daily or weekly audit to notice patterns without judgment.

The screen-time checklist

Most people have no idea how they actually use their phone. They know it feels like "too much," but they cannot name the when, where, or why. This checklist creates that visibility.

How to use it

  • Complete this checklist once a day for three days. Morning, midday, or evening — whatever you will actually do.
  • Be honest. There is no score. You are gathering data about yourself.
  • After three days, look for patterns. When do you pick up your phone most? What triggers it?
  • Use the patterns to adjust your Phone-Use Agreement.
Common trigger patterns

Boredom: Empty moments feel uncomfortable. The phone is an escape.

Anxiety: Checking the phone provides a false sense of control.

Transition: Moving between tasks feels hard. The phone is a procrastination bridge.

Social: FOMO, loneliness, or the need for connection drives compulsive checking.

The App Audit

Which apps serve you and which ones drain you.

The app audit

Not all screen time is equal. Some apps give you energy, information, or connection. Others take without giving back. This audit helps you tell the difference.

The three categories

Green apps — Serve me

Apps you use intentionally, that leave you feeling better, and that have a clear purpose. Examples: meditation apps, maps, work tools, messaging close friends.

Yellow apps — Sometimes serve me

Apps with a valid purpose but a high risk of overuse. Examples: email, news, social media with real connections, entertainment.

Red apps — Drain me

Apps you use compulsively, that leave you feeling worse, and that have no clear purpose in your life. Examples: infinite scroll feeds, comparison-driven social media, games with addictive mechanics.

What to do with the categories

  • Green: Keep. Protect. Use freely.
  • Yellow: Restrict. Set time limits. Use with intention.
  • Red: Delete, hide, or heavily restrict. These are the apps that most often break your boundaries.

Distraction-Interruption Cards

Physical cards to place around your workspace as visual reminders and friction creators.

Distraction-interruption cards

These cards are designed to be printed, cut out, and placed in strategic locations: your desk, your bedside table, your kitchen counter, your car dashboard. They create visual friction — a moment of pause between impulse and action.

How to use them

  1. Print the cards on the following pages.
  2. Cut them out along the dotted lines.
  3. Place them where you most often pick up your phone without thinking.
  4. When you see a card, pause. Ask the question on the card. Answer honestly.
  5. If the answer is "no reason," put the phone down. If there is a real reason, use it with intention.
Why physical cards work

Digital reminders are too easy to swipe away. A physical card in your environment creates a real-world pause that a notification cannot. It interrupts the automatic loop: trigger → reach for phone → scroll.

The cards

  • The Pause Card — For your desk. Asks: "Why am I picking this up?"
  • The Bedside Card — For your nightstand. Asks: "Is this more important than sleep?"
  • The Transition Card — For doorways and entrances. Asks: "Am I escaping or arriving?"
  • The Focus Card — For your workspace. States: "This block is phone-free."

Emergency Protocols

For when you pick up your phone without thinking and need a way back.

Emergency protocols

These protocols are for the moments when you realize you have been scrolling for twenty minutes and have no idea how you got there. They are not about prevention. They are about recovery.

A
The Scroll Spiral

You opened one app. Now you are deep in a feed you never intended to open.

  1. Don't shame yourself. It happens.
  2. Ask: "What was I doing before I picked up my phone?"
  3. Close the app. Not minimize. Close.
  4. Put the phone face-down, out of reach.
  5. Do one physical action: stand, stretch, drink water.
  6. Decide: return to the original task, or take a real break.
B
The Phantom Check

You checked your phone for a notification. There was none. You checked again. Still none. You keep checking.

  1. Recognize the loop: notification anxiety is a real trigger.
  2. Turn the phone face-down. The screen cannot call to you if you cannot see it.
  3. Set a "check again" timer for 30 minutes. Honor it.
  4. Ask: "Is there a real reason I need to know this now?" Usually, no.
C
The Bedtime Hijack

You got in bed to sleep. You checked one thing. It is now an hour later.

  1. Put the phone on the charger — in another room if possible.
  2. Do not use your phone as an alarm. Buy a physical alarm clock.
  3. If you need wind-down, use a book, not a screen.
  4. If you absolutely must use your phone, enable grayscale mode and night mode. Make the screen as unappealing as possible.

Your phone is a tool, not a trap

The designers of your phone want your attention. That is their job. Your job is to decide when and how you give it.

This kit is not about becoming someone who never uses their phone. It is about becoming someone who uses their phone on purpose — with boundaries, with awareness, and with the ability to recover when autopilot takes over.

What to remember

Boundaries, not bans. Complete phone abstinence is rarely sustainable. Boundaries are.

Environment shapes behavior. Physical cards, phone placement, and app organization matter more than willpower.

Recovery is part of the system. You will slip. The protocols exist because slipping is normal.

One boundary at a time. Don't overhaul everything. Pick one agreement, one card, one protocol. Build from there.

"You are not weak for struggling with your phone. You are human, and your phone was designed by people who understand human attention better than almost anyone on earth. Boundaries are not a sign of failure. They are a sign of self-respect."
