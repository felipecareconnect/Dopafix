How to use this planner

Time blindness means time feels abstract. Hours disappear without warning. Deadlines arrive as surprises. This planner makes time visible, concrete, and external — so your brain doesn't have to hold it all.

The core idea

Instead of a to-do list, you create time containers. Each block has a start time, an end time, and one job. When the block ends, you stop — even if you're not finished. This protects against hyperfocus spirals and forgotten transitions.

Before you begin

  • Use a visual timer alongside these templates — a physical sand timer, a color-changing light, or a timer app with a large display.
  • Don't plan every minute. Leave buffer blocks. Transitions take longer than you think.
  • Start with just the morning template for one week. Add the others only when the first feels natural.
  • Color-coding helps: one color for focus, one for breaks, one for transitions.
"The goal is not to control every minute. The goal is to make time feel real enough that you can work with it instead of against it."

Transition Scripts

Pre-written transitions for the moments that usually get stuck.

Transition scripts

Transitions are where time blindness hits hardest. You are deep in one thing, and suddenly you are supposed to be doing another thing. These scripts give your brain a bridge.

How to use a transition script

Read the script aloud or in your head. Follow the steps in order. Don't skip. The ritual is what makes the transition work.

Work → Break

  1. Set a timer for the break duration.
  2. Write down exactly where you stopped. (One sentence.)
  3. Stand up and move at least three steps away from your workspace.
  4. Do one physical thing: stretch, get water, look out a window.
  5. When the timer ends, return and read your stop-sentence.

Break → Work

  1. Set a timer for the work block.
  2. Write the one task for this block at the top of a blank page or sticky note.
  3. Close all unrelated tabs, apps, and notifications.
  4. Take three slow breaths.
  5. Start with the smallest possible first step. (Open the document. Write one sentence.)

Work → Evening

  1. Set a "work is over" alarm.
  2. Write tomorrow's first task on a visible surface.
  3. Close all work tabs and documents.
  4. Change one physical thing: clothes, room, lighting.
  5. Say out loud: "Work is done. Tomorrow is tomorrow."

Emergency Protocols

For the days when time completely disappears.

Emergency protocols

Some days, no amount of planning helps. Time slips away, hyperfocus takes over, or you wake up already behind. These protocols are for those days.

Protocol A: The lost morning

You meant to start at 9. It is now 11:30. You feel guilty and paralyzed.

Step 1: Stop calculating how much time you "wasted." That math is not useful.

Step 2: Pick ONE thing that matters today. Just one.

Step 3: Give it 25 minutes. Set a timer. Start anywhere.

Step 4: After 25 minutes, decide: continue, switch, or stop. Any choice is valid.

Protocol B: The hyperfocus spiral

You sat down to answer one email. Four hours passed. You missed lunch.

Step 1: Set a recovery timer for 10 minutes. Stand up. Move.

Step 2: Eat something. Drink water. Your brain is depleted.

Step 3: Look at your time blocks. Cancel or shrink anything non-essential.

Step 4: For the rest of the day, use a body double or external timer. Don't trust yourself alone with the task.

Protocol C: The everything-is-urgent day

Everything feels due now. You are bouncing between tasks, finishing nothing.

Step 1: Write every task down. Get it out of your head.

Step 2: Circle the ONE task with the hardest deadline. That is your first block.

Step 3: Hide or mute everything else for 25 minutes.

Step 4: Repeat. One task at a time. No multitasking.

You don't need to feel time to manage it

Time blindness is real. It is not a character flaw. It is a neurological difference in how your brain processes temporal information.

These templates exist to externalize time — to make it visible, structured, and manageable without requiring you to "feel" it passing.

What to remember

Externalize everything. Timers, visual blocks, and written plans reduce the cognitive load of tracking time.

Transitions are non-negotiable. Every block needs a bridge. Skipping transitions leads to burnout.

One block at a time. Don't plan the whole day in your head. Just the next block.

Recovery is part of the system. Bad days don't break the system. The emergency protocols exist because they happen.

"Time is not something you need to master. It is something you need to partner with — on terms that work for your brain."
